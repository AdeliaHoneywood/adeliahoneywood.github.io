#### Migration Map of Least Sandpiper <i>Calidris minutilla</i>

<embed type="text/html" src="portfolio_posts/migration_carto_demo_adelia.html" width="800" height="800">

For birders in New York City, July and August are shorebird migration! The best site for observing shorebirds in NYC is at [Jamaica Bay National Wildlife Refuge’s](https://www.nps.gov/gate/learn/historyculture/jamaica-bay-wildlife-refuge.htm) East Pond, an important stopover site for migrating shorebirds to refuel on their southward journey. The Least Sandpiper (<i>Calidris minutilla</i>) is a tiny sandpiper, 5-6 inches long, that dependably shows up in Jamaica Bay each summer associating with flocks of Semipalmated Sandpipers (<i>Calidris pusilla</i>) who tend to exponentially outnumber it.

Least Sandpipers migrate in a broad front across all of North America, breed in the Arctic, and winter from the southern U.S. into northern South America (Nebel & Cooper, 2020). Many migrating shorebirds along the east coast demonstrate elliptical migration, meaning the southbound journey to their wintering grounds likely takes place over the Atlantic Ocean, while their northbound journey typically occurs overland entering along the south coast of the U.S.(McNeil & Burton, 1977; Nebel & Cooper, 2020). This is consistent with the fact that in New York City we see the most shorebirds in the summer and Least Sandpipers are rarely observed from November to April (eBird, 2021). 

The map bears this all out (except that there appears to be a gap in the data in June.) It shows higher numbers of observations of Least Sandpiper along the mid- to upper-Atlantic Coast in August and September than in April and May. (Note that there appears to be a gap in the data for the month of June). Using the slider, the map hints at the fact that spring migration from the wintering grounds begins via the southern U.S. and spreads to the coasts, without a similar retreat to the central U.S. in the fall. Least sandpiper may be declining, especially along the east coast (Nebel & Cooper, 2020). Jamaica Bay National Wildlife Refuge is part of the [Gateway National Recreation Area](https://www.nps.gov/gate/index.htm) with coastal sites that are important stopovers for migrating shorebirds.

The map visualizes data from the [Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF)](https://www.gbif.org/) and is based on human observations of occurrence of Least Sandpiper, not counts, from iNaturalist(https://www.inaturalist.org/) Research-grade Observations. This data is joined to ecoregions from the [Ecoregions of the World Dataset](https://www.resolve.ngo/projects/ecoregions-world) published by RESOLVE.

#### [75 years of rising temperatures at Jamaica Bay National Wildlife Refuge in New York City](portfolio_posts/Jamaica_Bay_Climate_Trend.html)
<img 
src="/img/nyc_temp_trend.jpeg"
alt="Plot of rising temperatures at Jamaica Bay National Wildlife Refuge since 1950">

The data for this graph was collected from [Climate Data Online](https://www.ncdc.noaa.gov/cdo-web/) published by NOAA's [National Centers for Environmental Information](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/) from a monitoring station located at [JFK Airport](https://www.ncdc.noaa.gov/cdo-web/datasets/GHCND/stations/GHCND:USW00094789/detail) adjacent to Jamaica Bay National Wildlife Refuge in New York City. The station is rated as having 100 percent complete data since the inception of monitoring in 1948. The data shows that average annual temperatures have increased by 0.23&deg; C per decade, which is slightly higher than the global rate of warming since 1982 of [0.20&deg; C per decade](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/understanding-climate/climate-change-global-temperature).

According to the National Park Service, [Jamaica Bay has lost 72% of its coastal salt marsh](https://www.nps.gov/im/ncbn/jamaica-bay.htm) in the last 100 years. Climate change induced effects such as rising sea levels, increased frequency and intensity of storm events, and increased coastal flooding have contributed to the erosion of salt marsh and its replacement by open water.
